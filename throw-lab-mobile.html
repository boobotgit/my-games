Throw Lab

Toss your phone onto something soft. It records what the motion sensors feel, then works out how fast it flew, how far it fell, how hard it spun and how hard it landed.

  • 1
    Stand over a couch or bed. Aim for a soft landing, and keep it low for your first few throws.
  • 2
    Press RECORD, then throw within 15 seconds.
  • 3
    Leave the phone where it lands. Once it's been still for a moment, you'll see the numbers.

How it works
How does it know it's in the air?

An accelerometer measures every force on the phone except gravity. Sitting on a table it feels the table pushing up (1 g). Once it's in the air, nothing is pushing on it, so it reads about 0 g. That's free fall, the same thing astronauts feel. Throw Lab looks for the stretch where the reading drops near zero and times it.

How is the drop height calculated?

Two pieces: how fast your hand launched it upward (from adding up the acceleration during the throw, called integration), and how long it was airborne. Gravity is a known constant, so the physics is: drop = ½·g·t² − vup·t. Throw it straight up and catch it at the same height, and the drop comes out as zero.

How does it measure spin?

A separate sensor, the gyroscope, measures how fast the phone rotates around each of its three axes in degrees per second. Adding that up over the flight gives the total number of turns. It can also tell a frisbee-style flat spin from an end-over-end flip.

Why might the numbers be a bit off?

The phone takes about 60 readings a second. A throw lasts about a quarter of a second, so the speed is worked out from roughly 15 readings, and small errors add up. A fast spin also confuses it: the accelerometer sits a few centimetres from the middle of the phone, so spinning presses it outward and hides the free-fall reading. Gentle, flat tosses give the cleanest numbers.
